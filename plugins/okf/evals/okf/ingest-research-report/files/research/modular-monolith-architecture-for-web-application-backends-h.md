# Modular monolith architecture for web application backends: how it works, strengths, weaknesses, when to use it, when not to use it, trade-offs versus alternative patterns, and real-world examples in 2025-2026 practice

> Generated by /ae-research → research on 2026-11-15

## Summary
A modular monolith is one deployable application whose code is split into modules by business domain, with boundaries that tooling is meant to enforce. Richards and Ford rate it high for cost, simplicity and modularity and low for scalability, elasticity and fault tolerance. Shopify's large Rails codebase is the main real-world case, and Segment and Prime Video went back to consolidated deployments to escape the costs of distributed systems. The evidence also shows how hard boundaries are to enforce in practice: Packwerk reported violations faster than teams could fix them, Ruby keeps all code globally reachable, and a Rails Core member (2024) knew of no app shipping fully isolated modules to production. Use it when the business needs atomic transactions across modules, when a team is small to mid-sized, or when the domain boundaries are still being worked out. Be wary of it when parts of the system need to scale or fail independently. Expect it not to fix cultural problems such as ownership, onboarding or slow CI.

## Findings
- **Definition: a modular monolith is a domain-partitioned monolith deployed as one unit (one deployment quantum), with all code powering a single application and boundaries enforced between domains.** — confidence: high
  - Evidence: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 2nd ed. (2025), as given in Ted Neward's notes: 'deployed as a single unit of software', 'domain-partitioned architecture', number of quanta 1. Shopify (2019): 'a system where all of the code powers a single application and there are strictly enforced boundaries between different domains.' Both 3-0.
  - Sources: https://research.tedneward.com/architecture/fundamentals-of-software-architecture.html, https://shopify.engineering/deconstructing-monolith-designing-software-maximizes-developer-productivity
- **Strengths and weaknesses per Richards & Ford: the main strengths are overall cost, simplicity and modularity, and deployability rates slightly above a layered architecture. Scalability, elasticity and fault tolerance each get 1 of 5 stars, because one failure, such as running out of memory, takes down the whole unit.** — confidence: medium
  - Evidence: A secondary summary of the 2025 book. Ratings: simplicity 5, responsiveness 4, modularity/maintainability/testability/deployability/evolvability 2 each, cost $. The text says 'monolithic deployments don't support fault tolerance.' The prose says testability rates higher than layered, but the ratings table shows both at 2, so don't treat testability as a firm advantage. Votes 3-0.
  - Sources: https://research.tedneward.com/architecture/fundamentals-of-software-architecture.html
- **The main long-term risk is growing too big to maintain, test and deploy. Warning signs: changes take too long, changes break unrelated areas, teams get in each other's way, and startup is slow. Too much code reuse blurs module boundaries, and heavy communication between modules suggests the domains were defined badly.** — confidence: medium
  - Evidence: Quoted from notes on the book (2nd ed., ch. 11). Secondary source, 3-0.
  - Sources: https://research.tedneward.com/architecture/fundamentals-of-software-architecture.html
- **When to use it: transactional integrity is a force for keeping functions together. If the business needs one atomic unit of work across functions, they belong in the same deployable. Teams that split services without first analyzing data dependencies often get stuck. A service-based architecture with one shared database is a practical middle ground.** — confidence: high
  - Evidence: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Thoughtworks podcast (Sept 2024). Richards: 'necessarily we have to join those services back together.' Ford: 'many teams have gone on a wild west adventure of breaking apart their services', which the data then would not allow. Sagas are an alternative only if the business accepts losing atomicity. Primary source, 3-0 on both claims.
  - Sources: https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/insights/podcasts/technology-podcasts/software-service-granularity-getting-it-right
- **Granularity versus modularity: splitting too finely produces Richards' 'grains of sand' anti-pattern, and teams then oscillate between splitting and re-merging ('rubber banding'). The guidance is 'Embrace modularity but beware of granularity.'** — confidence: high
  - Evidence: Richards coined the term in 2016 and restates it in the 2024 podcast. Primary source, 3-0.
  - Sources: https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/insights/podcasts/technology-podcasts/software-service-granularity-getting-it-right
- **Shopify's core Rails codebase (over a decade of work, more than 1,000 developers) is a modular monolith, chosen over microservices. Microservices would have brought multiple test and deploy pipelines, infrastructure overhead, harder data access, network latency and lower reliability on every call, and refactors that must be coordinated across services. Shopify argues that 'Distributing code over multiple machines is a topology change, not an architectural change': splitting into services does not fix poor boundaries.** — confidence: high
  - Evidence: Two first-party posts (2019, 2020), five claims merged, all 3-0. 'A poorly designed API within the monolith will still be a poorly designed API within a service.' Shopify still accepts microservices for components that are isolated and distinct enough, and runs some services outside the core, so this is not a blanket rejection.
  - Sources: https://shopify.engineering/deconstructing-monolith-designing-software-maximizes-developer-productivity, https://shopify.engineering/blogs/engineering/enforcing-modularity-rails-apps-packwerk, https://shopify.engineering/enforcing-modularity-rails-apps-packwerk
- **Enforcing boundaries is hard in practice. Shopify's first components split the code by commerce domain (Delivery, Online Store, Checkouts), and that split did not match how the code actually runs, so components became top-level groupings of packages. Packwerk, a static-analysis gem released in September 2020, found violations far faster than teams could, or cared to, fix them.** — confidence: high
  - Evidence: Shopify's own retrospective (Feb 2024): domains 'do not reflect the way Shopify's code actually functions in practice', and the violation rate 'vastly outpaced' teams' ability to fix. Packwerk 3.0 also dropped privacy checks. First-party, 3-0. The reorganization is described as ongoing.
  - Sources: https://shopify.engineering/a-packwerk-retrospective
- **Critique from inside Shopify: packaging a Rails monolith into engines or packages, for example with Packwerk, does not by itself reduce coupling, because Ruby code stays globally reachable. As of September 2024 the speaker knew of no application deploying fully isolated monolith modules to production. Shopify's only isolated component has no product functionality.** — confidence: medium
  - Evidence: Eileen Uchitelle (Rails Core, Shopify) keynote at Rails World 2024. The isolation claim passed only 2-1, because Packwerk can fail CI on new violations, so 'only reports' slightly understates it. The awareness claim passed 3-0 but rests on one person's knowledge. Primary but single source.
  - Sources: https://rubyevents.org/talks/opening-keynote-the-myth-of-the-modular-monolith
- **Modularization does not fix organizational and operational problems such as code ownership, onboarding, flaky tests or slow CI. The argument is that these are cultural problems: 'you cannot solve human problems with modularity.'** — confidence: medium
  - Evidence: Opinion from the same 2024 keynote, 3-0 on accurate reporting. She grants that boundaries make it somewhat easier to assign ownership. Shopify's earlier posts claim more organizational benefit, so practitioners disagree on this.
  - Sources: https://rubyevents.org/talks/opening-keynote-the-myth-of-the-modular-monolith
- **Segment case, consolidating microservices: per-destination microservices grew past 140 services with a repo each, and keeping them running took most of the time of 3 full-time engineers. After moving to one service and a monorepo, shared-library improvements rose from 32 to 46 within a year. The trade-off was weaker fault isolation: a crash in one destination's code could take the service down for all destinations.** — confidence: medium
  - Evidence: Segment engineering post (July 2018; the URL now redirects to the Twilio blog). Three claims, all 3-0. The velocity figure is a self-reported, undefined metric with no adjustment for other changes made at the same time, such as the shared-library versioning cleanup, so it shows correlation, not cause. The fault-isolation cost matches the book's 1-star rating.
  - Sources: https://segment.com/blog/goodbye-microservices
- **Prime Video case: its audio/video quality inspection service cut infrastructure costs by over 90% by moving from distributed serverless (Step Functions, Lambda, S3 for intermediate data) to a single-process application on ECS/EC2 that moves data in memory.** — confidence: medium
  - Evidence: InfoQ (May 2023), reporting Prime Video's March 2023 tech blog. 3-0. This is a narrow, high-volume media pipeline, not a general verdict. Detectors were still split across multiple ECS tasks to scale. No baseline dollar figures were published.
  - Sources: https://infoq.com/news/2023/05/prime-ec2-ecs-saves-costs
- **JVM tooling: Spring Modulith (announced in October 2022 as experimental, now a full Spring project) treats each direct sub-package of the main application package as a module. Nested packages are internal. ArchUnit-based verification in tests rejects cycles between modules and access to another module's internals, and @ApplicationModule(allowedDependencies=...) can restrict which modules a module may depend on.** — confidence: high
  - Evidence: Official announcement by the project lead. Two claims, 3-0. Enforcement happens at test or verification time, not in the compiler. Later 1.x/2.x versions add @NamedInterface and open modules to relax the defaults.
  - Sources: https://spring.io/blog/2022/10/21/introducing-spring-modulith

## Caveats
Most evidence comes from a few sources: Shopify (three first-party posts plus one keynote), one Thoughtworks podcast, and one book that I saw only through Ted Neward's secondary notes, not the book itself. The case studies date from 2018 to 2024 (Segment 2018, Shopify 2019/2020/2024, Prime Video 2023). Nothing in this set covers 2025-2026 adoption data or new examples beyond the 2025 book edition. Segment's and Prime Video's figures are self-reported, have no independent check, and say nothing about causation. Prime Video is a serverless-to-single-process consolidation, not strictly a modular monolith. The book's 'testability' advantage contradicts its own ratings table. The Packwerk isolation claim passed only 2-1, and two claims describing Packwerk's mechanics were refuted 0-3, so details of how Packwerk enforces boundaries are uncertain. No sources covered .NET or other ecosystems' tooling (for example ABP or Modular Monolith templates), Go or Node practice, or quantitative surveys.
